## Abstract 

We propose StreamVLO, a streaming visual–LiDAR odome-try framework that performs unified spatio-temporal corre-lation with Mamba models and tackles the long-standing cumulative drift problem via an online Cumulative Drift Compensation scheme for localization in 4D dynamic en-vironments. Specifically, StreamVLO introduces a uni-fied spatio-temporal correlation module built on Mamba to fuse heterogeneous visual and LiDAR cues across multi-frame clips, overcoming the limited temporal exploration of prior pairwise methods. Furthermore, a Cumulative Drift Compensation module minimizes cumulative drift by iteratively learning residual corrections from multiple his-torical frames in a causal manner. To strengthen spa-tial feature representation on salient regions, we adopt a Keypoint-Aware Auxiliary Loss with a winner-takes-all strategy. StreamVLO achieves state-of-the-art performance on two commonly used autonomous driving datasets, re-ducing errors by 19% ( trel ) and 22% ( rrel ) on KITTI, and by 18% ATE and 16% RPE on Argoverse, while remaining suitable for real-time deployment.

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Visual-LiDAR Odometry 

Visual-LiDAR odometry combines the strengths of visual and LiDAR sensors, leveraging 2D texture [6, 32] and 3D geometric [35, 46, 50, 61, 62] information to improve pose estimation. Previous approaches are broadly classified into loosely and tightly integrated methods. Loosely integrated methods [22, 30, 64, 90] use LiDAR data to enhance depth estimation and visual data for pose tracking. However, these approaches rely on interpolation between 3D points and 2D pixels, which introduces potential errors due to the inac-curate point-to-pixel correspondences. Tightly integrated methods [2, 66, 89, 93] aim for a seamless fusion of visual and LiDAR data to enhance consistency. For the learning-based methods, MVL-SLAM [2] fuses RGB images and Li-DAR using RCNNs. LIP-Loc [66] applies contrastive learn-ing for cross-modal localization. Nevertheless, they strug-gle with the structural differences between point clouds and images. Recent works [34, 48] introduce descriptor fusion or clustering techniques to further improve the structural alignment. However, these approaches often suffer from increasing computational costs due to the additional feature processing, limiting their real-time applicability in practi-cal scenarios. Moreover, previous methods are commonly constrained by pairwise inputs, which, however, lack multi-frame temporal information. To our knowledge, our work is the first visual–LiDAR odometry method designing De-formable Mamba and Temporal Mamba for efficient query-based fusion across multiple frames, thereby enabling a uni-fied multi-modal representation. 

2.2. Memory Designs for Autonomous Driving 

Memory-based temporal modeling has become essen-tial for handling occlusions, sensor failures, and complex scenes in single-frame autonomous driving models [11, 26, 29, 40, 41, 52, 53, 82, 83]. Recent architectures fo-cus on feature retention [39] and pose estimation [28, 33], both crucial for autonomous driving applications. In the feature domain, for example, BEVDet4D [29] and BEV-Formerv2 [82] integrate features from multiple frames with inefficient long-term modeling. VideoBEV [26] improves upon this by recurrently propagating BEV raster queries across time, enabling progressive accumulation of tempo-ral information. In the vectorized domain, Sparse4Dv2 [40] utilizes an RNN-based structure for object-level queries, while Sparse4Dv3 [41] introduces temporal denoising to improve consistency across frames. These memory mecha-nisms have also influenced high-definition vectorized map-ping approaches [83]. Inspired by these prior advancements, our framework incorporates both an implicit feature embedding memory bank and an explicit vector pose memory bank to effec-tively capture and store temporal cues from history frames. 2.3. Temporal Modeling with Mamba 

Sequential modeling is central to trajectory forecasting and end-to-end autonomous driving. In time, encoders/decoders often adopt recurrent architectures [71] or Transformer-based frameworks [10, 51, 60, 72] to capture temporal pat-terns. Recently, state space models (SSMs) [24, 25] have emerged as a strong alternative [27]. Mamba [23] intro-duces selective SSMs with time-varying parameters for ef-ficient training/inference. Extensions include 2D SSMs [4] within Transformer blocks [17], VMamba [56] that se-quences image patches for global receptive fields with near-linear complexity. Subsequent works [38, 47, 49] also gen-eralize it to higher-dimensional data. SSMs have also been integrated into diffusion models to replace Transformer-based UNets, e.g., DiffuSSM [81] retains high-resolution features without coarse compression. Drawing on these developments, we employ Mamba to manage both explicit vector pose memory and implicit fea-ture memory, enabling robust temporal modeling. 

## 3. Method 

The architecture of StreamVLO is shown in Fig. 2. StreamVLO begins with the Unified Spatio-Temporal Cor-relation (Sec. 3.1) with deformable spatial Mamba and tem-poral Mamba for streaming sequences, which integrates vi-sual and LiDAR features across frames into a unified repre-sentation. This unified representation is utilized to predict the pose estimates. In addition, the predicted pose is com-pensated with long-term temporal information using differ-entiable Cumulative Drift Compensation (Sec. 3.2) to mit-igate error accumulation in an online, causal fashion, re-fining the final pose. The loss functions, including our de-signed Keypoint-Aware Auxiliary Loss with a winner-takes-all strategy, are specified in Sec. 3.3. Before we delve into the detailed model design, we fol-low [44, 48, 74] for point encoding and [31] for image encoding to extract hierarchical features for both modali-ties. 3D points are first projected onto a cylindrical sur-face [74] to organize the originally irregular points, where the original 3D coordinates are further filled in correspond-ing projected 2D positions to preserve raw point geome-try, converting the point cloud into pseudo-images of size 

HP × WP × 3. Then these pseudo-images are passed to a point encoder [74], producing multi-scale point features represented as FP ∈ RHP ×WP ×D , where D is the fea-ture dimension. The 2D input images I ∈ RH×W ×3 are processed through a convolutional feature pyramid network [48], generating multi-level image features represented as 

FI ∈ RH×W ×C , where H, W , and C correspond to the height, width, and channels, respectively. 

3.1. Unified Spatio-Temporal Correlation 

We design a Unified Spatio-Temporal Correlation block consisting of MaxPooling, Mamba [23], and gMLP [43] (MMG ) to extract spatial and temporal representations of point and image features causally across incoming frames. Fig. 3 shows the detailed network structure of MMG . It harmonizes diverse input representations across different modalities and temporal dimensions: The gMLP encodes sequential information into a unified feature space, pro-viding a foundation for learning from varied data sources. Mamba then establishes temporal interactions within the se-quences, capturing long-term dependencies across frames. We extend the Mamba structure into a Deformable Mamba 

for spatial modeling and a Temporal Mamba for tempo-ral modeling, in order to achieve effective and efficient modality-agnostic modeling by harnessing diverse input representations across modalities and temporal dimensions, which are detailed below. 4.2. Implementation Details 

Data Pre-processing. Following the sparse sampling ap-proach for LiDAR points from [74], we also design a fusion mask that flags LiDAR queries that can interact with image features, given the substantial difference in spatial range be-tween the LiDAR and camera data. 

Hyper-parameters. We use the Adam optimizer with β1 =0.9 and β2 = 0.999 . The initial learning rate is 0.001 and decays exponentially every 13 epochs until reaching 0.00001. We use a batch size of 8. The values for αi across different module outputs are set to 1.6, 0.8, 1.6, and 1.6, respectively. The learnable parameters kt and kq are initial-ized to 0.0 and -2.5, respectively. For each feature level, the number of LiDAR queries is set to 116, 228, 904, and 3600. During training, the KITTI sequences 00-06 are divided into video clips with a duration of  TC = 60  frames (6s), with further segmentation into sub-clips of  Ts = 3  frames (0.3s) for the collective average loss calculation. The maximum history length  Th and the compensation interval  Tg are set as 30 and 20 respectively. The value of top-k is set as 100 for the winner-takes-all loss.

Comparison with Traditional SLAMs on KITTI. To further validate the efficacy of our method in long-term temporal modeling for drift mitigation, we compare StreamVLO with traditional visual SLAM systems (Ta-ble 2) and traditional visual–LiDAR SLAM systems (Ta-ble 3). StreamVLO outperforms these methods on most se-quences in terms of Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) and relative translation errors, demonstrating its effectiveness in mitigating drift. Notably, these SLAM systems rely on global optimization and loop closure across a sequence of frames to reduce cumulative errors, whereas our method leverages Cumulative Drift Compensation to backtrack pre-viously observed frames and compensate for cumulative er-rors, without requiring explicit mapping or loop closure. This property makes StreamVLO more adaptable to se-quences of arbitrary lengths in driving scenes.

## 5. Conclusion 

In this work, we introduced StreamVLO, a visual–LiDAR odometry framework designed to address the challenges like accumulative drifts. By integrating a unified MMG architecture, StreamVLO combines visual and LiDAR data, enabling robust feature fusion while capturing essential spatiotemporal dependencies. Our model leverages a Cumulative Drift Compensation module to minimize cumulative drifts. Moreover, a Keypoint-Aware Auxiliary Loss is proposed to enhance spatial feature representations in high-salience regions. Exten-sive experiments demonstrate that StreamVLO achieves state-of-the-art performance in both KITTI and Argov-erse datasets, while maintaining real-time inference latency. 39093 References
