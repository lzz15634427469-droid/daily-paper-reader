# Abstract 

Remote pose estimation of noncooperative Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) from imagery is critical, as they cannot be influenced or instrumented in advance. Deep-learning-based approaches offer a promising solution; however, their development is constrained by the cost and difficulty of acquiring large-scale real-world datasets with accurate pose labels. Synthetic imagery provides an alter-native, but models trained on synthetic data must overcome the synthetic-to-real domain gap to generalize to real-world imagery. This work investigates the inherent synthetic-to-real generalization capability of a Vision Transformer (ViT)-based model for monocular UAV pose estimation. The proposed approach employs a self-supervised DINOv2 backbone and is trained exclusively on labeled synthetic imagery while being evaluated on labeled real-world im-agery. Pose ambiguity-aware strategies are incorporated during training and inference to address ambiguities aris-ing from the projection of a three-dimensional target onto a two-dimensional image plane and from target symmetries. An α–β filter is further integrated during inference to im-prove pose estimations. To assess the model under opera-tional requirements, it is evaluated in terms of Mean Angu-lar Error (MAE) and inference time, both before and after filtering, using a real-world dataset containing 77,077 la-beled UAV images. Before filtering, the model achieves an MAE of 19 .18 o and an inference time of 13 .25 ms, whereas after filtering, these values are 8.74 o and 13 .42 ms, respec-tively. 

# 1. Introduction 

Remote pose estimation of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) from imagery is essential for applications such as autonomous navigation [14], aerial refueling [4], and directed-energy systems [9]. The latter application moti-

vates this work, where accurate pose estimation of non-cooperative UAVs is critical to automate operational sce-narios that are difficult or impractical for human operators. Since noncooperative targets cannot be instrumented or in-fluenced in advance, camera-based remote sensing provides an attractive approach for estimating UAV pose from im-agery. To estimate pose from this imagery, deep learning-based methods have emerged as a powerful solution due to their ability to learn visual representations directly from image data. Deep-learning-based pose estimation models have shown significant promise in recent years [5, 10, 12]. How-ever, their performance is often limited by the availability of labeled training data. Collecting and annotating large-scale real-world data, especially in the noncooperative UAV context is costly and time-consuming, particularly when ac-curate pose labels are required. Synthetic data generation provides an attractive alternative by producing large quanti-ties of labeled imagery under controlled variations such as pose, lighting, and viewing conditions. Nevertheless, mod-els trained on synthetic data must overcome the synthetic-to-real domain gap to generalize effectively to real-world imagery. Similar domain gap challenges have been observed in satellite pose estimation. The second international Satel-lite Pose Estimation Competition (SPEC2021), co-hosted by the European Space Agency (ESA) and Stanford Univer-sity’s Space Rendezvous Laboratory (SLAB), highlighted the challenge of generalizing models trained on synthetic imagery to realistic imagery [13], motivating research into methods for bridging the synthetic-to-real domain gap. Al-though focused primarily on satellite pose estimation, the underlying problem is also relevant to noncooperative UAV pose estimation, where acquiring large quantities of accu-rately labeled real-world pose data is costly and often im-practical. This work investigates whether a Vision Transformer (ViT)-based model can generalize from synthetic to real UAV imagery for pose estimation. The proposed model is based on the self-supervised DINOv2 [11] and is trained arXiv:2610.04335v1 [cs.CV] 3 Oct 2026 exclusively on synthetic imagery and evaluated on real-world imagery for direct pose estimation. This experimen-tal setting is intentionally adopted to assess the inherent synthetic-to-real generalization capability of the ViT-based model. Techniques such as adversarial training [3] and do-main randomization [16] could be employed to reduce the synthetic-to-real domain gap. However, the primary interest of this study is the inherent synthetic-to-real performance of the ViT-based model without incorporating explicit domain-gap reduction techniques. ViT leverages self-attention mechanisms to capture global contextual information [1]. ViT has demonstrated strong performance across a wide range of computer vi-sion tasks. However, its effectiveness for synthetic-to-real UAV pose estimation remains insufficiently explored. In this work, the proposed ViT-based model is designed to ac-count for pose ambiguity, a significant challenge in monoc-ular pose estimation arising from the projection of a three-dimensional target onto a two-dimensional image plane and from target symmetries. As illustrated in Figure 1, distinct target poses may produce nearly identical image appear-ances, potentially leading to large pose prediction errors. To mitigate this issue, ambiguity-aware strategies are incor-porated during both training and inference. The proposed model is evaluated using two criteria that are critical for practical deployment: prediction accuracy, quantified by mean angular error, and inference time. In addition, an α − β filter is integrated during inference to smooth the estimations. Accuracy and inference time are evaluated before and after filtering to quantify its effect. The UAV imagery used in this research consists of la-beled synthetic and real-world datasets. The synthetic dataset was generated in simulation using a 3D UAV virtual model, whereas the labeled real-world dataset was acquired using a laboratory testbed with 3D printed UAV model, as detailed in [7]. A total of 77,077 labeled real-world images were used in this study.

Backbone. The feature extractor is DINOv2-Large [11], a ViT-L/14 [2] with 24 blocks, hidden width 1024 , 16 heads, and ∼ 304 M parameters, pretrained by self-supervised dis-tillation on LVD-142M, a curated collection of 142 million unlabeled images [11]. It was selected for three reasons: its features transfer across visual domains without target-domain labels, which matches the synthetic-to-real setting of this work; it outputs features as a sequence that a query-based decoder can attend to directly; and self-attention lets every patch see the whole aircraft, so that the relationship between, for example, the nose and a far wingtip is available in a single layer. Every input crop, whatever its original size (200 ×200 in the reported experiments; Sec. 4.2), is resized to 336 ×336 before entering the backbone, which divides it into 576 patch features of width 1024 ; after a LayerNorm these form the memory Z that both decoder stages attend to. All 24 blocks are fine-tuned, because a frozen encoder’s features are nearly identical for an image and its mirror im-age, and telling those apart is precisely what the model must learn. 

Why keypoints, and why in two stages. Telling the left wing from the right wing in the image is the specific job the keypoints exist to do, and it is worth stating why the network cannot do it directly. DINOv2 is pretrained with augmentations that include horizontal flips, which teach the encoder that an image and its mirror should map to the same representation. Its patch features are therefore close to mirror-invariant by construction, and a single-pass decoder reading them is being asked to name the left wing from ev-idence that does not distinguish left from right. Fine-tuning pushes the encoder away from that invariance (Sec. 4.3), but slowly and incompletely. Keypoints provide a way around this, because the seven keypoints are not equally affected by a mirror flip. The nose, the tail, and the fin each have no mirror counterpart: each is a single part lying on the aircraft’s plane of symme-try, so a mirror flip cannot confuse it with another keypoint. Consistent with this, these three were observed during de-velopment to be localized accurately even on images whose orientation output was completely wrong. The wingtips and horizontal tail surfaces, in contrast, come in left/right pairs, and a mirror swaps each with its partner; identifying the left wing is therefore exactly the question the mirror makes ambiguous. Stage 1 accordingly predicts only the three un-paired keypoints. Together they define the body axis, run-ning from nose to tail, and the side of that axis on which the fin appears in the image. That side does flip under a mirror, so it carries the left/right information the backbone features lack. Stage 2 receives it as input and uses it to assign the four paired keypoints consistently with the fin side. This settles which wingtip is which in the image. It does not de-termine which of the four candidate orientations of Sec. 2.3 is the true one, because all four present the fin on the same side of the body axis; that choice is left to the radar cue at inference (Sec. 3.3). Figure 3 shows the two groups on a sample image. Each stage is four transformer decoder layers with eight heads and a small set of learned queries, one per keypoint it must locate, plus a rotation query in stage 2; each query attends to the other queries and to Z, and a small head reads out its image coordinate, or, for the rotation query, the six-dimensional rotation of Eq. (3) and a left/right (chirality) logit. The keypoint loss supervises all seven keypoints, so stage 1 is trained through the same objective rather than by a separate detector.

The sixth entry of c is a saturated function of the variable p

rather than an independent quantity. The vector is defined this way because it captures, in the fewest numbers, three things stage 2 needs and cannot easily get from the features alone. First, u and ℓ give the body’s direction and apparent length, so the rotation query starts from a coarse orienta-tion and a foreshortening estimate rather than from nothing. Second, and most important, the sign of p, whether the fin is to the left or right of the body line, is the one quantity in the image that flips under a mirror while u does not. It is there-fore the single piece of evidence that separates an aircraft from its mirror image, and it is computable from three key-points the network finds reliably. Because the fin sits very close to the body line, p is a small number; the saturated copy tanh(25 p) passes its sign through at full strength so that stage 2 receives a clear left-or-right signal regardless of how small the offset is. The cue is concatenated onto every stage-2 query through a learned projection, so each query begins its attention already knowing the body axis and the fin side. The benefit is that an ambiguity the backbone fea-tures cannot resolve becomes an explicit input: stage 2 can assign the wings conditioned on the fin side rather than hav-ing to infer the image’s handedness from features that are nearly mirror-invariant. Because the cue is a deterministic function of stage-1 output, it adds no parameters and can-not overfit; it only routes information already in the image to the queries that need it. The separate chirality head gives the network a direct, low-dimensional target for the same left/right decision, which stabilizes training of the mirror-sensitive queries. 

Teacher forcing. Early in training the stage-1 keypoints are inaccurate, so a cue computed from them is mostly noise, and stage 2 can learn to ignore it entirely. To pre-vent this, the cue is computed during training from either the ground-truth keypoints or the stage-1 predictions, cho-sen independently for each sample: with probability ρ the ground truth is used, and otherwise the prediction. The quantity ρ is a sampling probability, not an error measure. It is decayed linearly from 1.0 to 0.25 over the first 60% 

of epochs and held at 0.25 thereafter, so stage 2 first learns to use a clean cue and then adapts to the noise of the real stage-1 output. At inference the cue always comes from the stage-1 predictions.

3.2. Dataset Augmentation 

The model is trained on synthetic images and evaluated on real SWIR imagery, so its real-world accuracy depends on how closely the synthetic appearance matches the sensor. Each synthetic training image is a binary silhouette mask: a uniform white aircraft on a black background. A real SWIR image of the same aircraft is far from binary, so the gap to be closed is one of intra-silhouette appearance , meaning how the sensor fills in and blurs a shape, rather than of texture or lighting. Closing it therefore does not require a photore-alistic renderer, only a statistical model of what the sensor does to a silhouette. Three such models are compared in this work; they are described below and summarized in Tab. 1, and Fig. 4 shows the effect of the one adopted.

# 4. Experimental Setup 

4.1. Dataset 

The real dataset contains 77 ,077 labeled SWIR images col-lected using the testbed described in [6]. A 3D-printed fixed-wing UAV model is mounted on a three-axis gimbal and imaged with a SWIR tracking camera. The gimbal mo-tors are stepped through a dense grid of angular positions, and each image is assigned an orientation from the com-manded motor positions. This provides hardware-captured images with precise ground truth. Further details on the testbed, motor position grid, and labeling procedure are given in [6]. Figure 5 shows an example real image along-side a synthetic sample. 

4.2. Synthetic data and the synthetic-to-real proto-col 

The task is intrinsically a synthetic-to-real transfer problem: labels are cheap and exact in simulation but costly to ob-tain at scale on hardware, so the model is trained purely on synthetic renderings and evaluated purely on real sensor imagery. Training uses 100 ,000 synthetic images (binary silhouette masks rendered from the target geometry over a dense grid of orientations), randomly partitioned into train-ing, validation, and test sets in the proportions 80 /10 /10 .Ground-truth orientations are provided as axis-angle vec-tors (Sec. 2.1), and the images are made sensor-realistic by the calibrated appearance model of Sec. 3.2. Evalua-tion uses the 77 ,077 real SWIR images of Sec. 4.1. No real image is ever seen in training; every reported real-image number is a measurement of sim-to-real generalization.

4.3. Implementation details 

We use AdamW with a batch size of 32 and train the model in two phases. In phase 1, the backbone is frozen and only the decoder and heads are trained for two epochs with a learning rate of 10 −3. The chirality term is disabled dur-ing this phase because the frozen self-supervised features are nearly identical for an image and its mirror, making the chirality bit difficult to learn. In phase 2, all 24 back-bone blocks are unfrozen and the chirality term is enabled. The backbone and decoder use separate learning rates of 

3×10 −6 and 2×10 −4, respectively. The smaller learning rate for the backbone allows it to adapt toward mirror-sensitive features while preserving the pretrained represen-tation. Both learning rates follow the same schedule: they increase linearly to their full values over the first three epochs, remain constant through epoch 110 , and then decay according to a cosine schedule toward 10% of their initial values, reached at epoch 150 .Training runs for up to 150 epochs, with early stopping if the synthetic validation loss does not improve for 20 con-secutive epochs. The reported model is the checkpoint with the lowest synthetic validation loss, which occurs at epoch 

102 . Real images are not used either for training or for checkpoint selection. The resulting model has 441 .4M pa-rameters and is trained on a single NVIDIA L40S GPU. During evaluation, the 77 ,077 real images are distributed across eight GPUs and processed in parallel, reducing eval-uation time without changing the results. Table 2. 4.4. Evaluation protocol 

For each real image, we compute the pose prediction and the radar-cue-corrected prediction described in Sec. 3.3. The geodesic error of each prediction relative to the ground-truth orientation is then computed using Eq. (2). We report the mean and median geodesic error, along with the fraction of predictions with errors greater than 90 ◦. The mean and me-dian describe the overall and typical prediction errors, re-spectively, while the 90 ◦ threshold captures severe failures in which the estimated orientation is far from the ground truth. The evaluation is performed on all 77 ,077 real SWIR images using 200 ×200 crops.

What remains at 180 ◦. If the two bits resolve the nose– tail and top–bottom ambiguities, why does any error near 

180 ◦ survive? The bits are never undefined: by Eq. (5), nts and tbs always take a value in {+1 , −1}. What fails is relia-bility, not definition. 5.4. Sequential α-β refinement 

The 4.1% of single-frame predictions beyond 90 ◦ are the residual mirror flips the radar cue cannot fix. Because the real images are captured as a raster scan of the gimbal mo-tors in 3◦ steps, consecutive frames are close in orientation, which a constant-rate temporal filter can exploit to suppress isolated errors; this is also representative of deployment, where a maneuvering target traces a continuous attitude tra-jectory. The α-β filter of [8] is adopted and applied as a post-hoc smoother with an outlier gate on the radar-cue-corrected Rout of Eq. (12).

5.6. Qualitative failure analysis 

Figure 7 shows four representative frames: two accurate predictions (sub-degree error) and two catastrophic failures (∼ 180 ◦). Two factors distinguish them. First, the failures sit near the degenerate viewing configuration where (Re 1)z

or (Re 3)z approaches zero: the fuselage is heavily fore-shortened, the wings and tail collapse toward the same sil-houette under the reflections of Eq. (4), and the geometric cue of Eq. (6) becomes noise-dominated because the fin off-set p approaches zero. The accurate cases are viewed near broadside or at moderate angles where all parts are sepa-rately resolvable. Second, the failures are viewed at higher tilt, where illumination strikes the airframe obliquely rather than perpendicular to its surface, producing nonuniform re-flectance across the fuselage; the accurate cases, viewed closer to broadside, receive more uniform illumination and present cleaner features to the network. 

# 6. Conclusion 

This work investigated the synthetic-to-real generalization capability of a ViT-based model for monocular pose esti-mation of a noncooperative UAV. Using a self-supervised DINOv2 backbone, the model was trained exclusively on labeled synthetic imagery and evaluated on 77,077 labeled real-world UAV images. Pose ambiguity-aware strategies were incorporated during training and inference, and an α–

β filter was used to further improve pose estimates. The model achieved a mean angular error of 19 .18 o with an in-ference time of 13 .25 ms before filtering, and 8.74 o and 

13 .42 ms, respectively, after filtering. The mean angular error of 8.74 o is considered accept-able given three identified factors that contribute to this er-ror: the synthetic-to-real domain gap, pose misalignment in the real-world dataset resulting from experimental imper-fections, and the inherent prediction error of deep-learning models. Considering these factors, the results demonstrate the potential of the proposed model for real-world pose es-timation of noncooperative UAVs. Future work will investigate further domain-gap reduc-tion techniques, including the use of photogrammetry to re-construct a 3D virtual model directly from a real UAV. This approach could enable the generation of virtually unlimited labeled training imagery that retains the visual characteris-tics of the real UAV, thereby useful to reduce the synthetic-to-real domain gap.
